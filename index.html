How much could my money become?

Split your savings between a risky part (shares from around the world) and a safer part (euro government bonds), and see the whole range of things that could happen — not just one hopeful number.

Every outcome on this page is built from real market history going back to 1900. Nothing is invented, and nothing is a promise.

    What is being applied

      This is a tax calculator, not a tax adviser. It uses the headline rates for a straightforward private investor holding two ordinary UCITS ETFs, and it cannot know your situation. Three real complications it deliberately ignores:
      • In Italy the reduced 12.5% rate applies to the government-bond part of the return only if the fund actually holds white-list government bonds. A synthetic bond ETF, which tracks the index through a swap instead of owning the bonds, can lose that treatment and be taxed entirely at 26%.
      • In the UK the numbers here assume both funds have UK reporting status. A fund without it is taxed on the whole gain as income at your marginal rate, which is far worse than the capital-gains treatment shown.
      • Rates, bands and allowances change. These are the ones in force for the 2026 Italian year and the 2026/27 UK year, held constant for the whole period — which no government has ever actually done.

      How the fee is applied

        This says nothing about whether a consultant is worth it. It prices the fee, which is the easy half. A good adviser can be worth several times this number — by stopping you selling in a crash, by getting the tax wrapper right, by making you save at all. A bad one costs you this and the mistakes. Two things worth knowing before you judge the figure:
        • A percentage of everything you hold is charged whether the year was good or bad, and it grows as your pot grows, so the bill in the last year is usually several times the bill in the first. Fixed fees, hourly advice and one-off financial plans do not behave like this at all.
        • In Italy a consultant's fee is normally paid from your own pocket or debited from the account, and it does not reduce the taxable gain — the model treats it the way it treats the funds' own charge, taking it out of the pot before tax is worked out. If yours is paid separately, the money leaves your pocket instead and the tax bill above is slightly understated.

        8 · How this works, in plain language for somebody who knows nothing about finance

        The two things you are choosing between

        A world share ETF is a single, cheap fund that buys a tiny piece of thousands of companies in dozens of countries. You are not betting on one company; you are betting that companies in general keep making money. Over long stretches this has paid well — about +5% a year above inflation since 1900 — but the ride is violent. Its worst single year in this dataset was 2008 at −40%.

        Euro government bonds means lending money to governments like Germany, France, Italy and Spain, which pay you interest. In a normal year this barely moves. But bonds are not a safe box: they lose value when interest rates rise, and inflation quietly eats them. Over the whole period since 1900 euro-area government bonds returned −0.9% a year after inflation — the two world wars destroyed bondholders twice. Their worst single year here was 1923 at −40%.

        So why hold bonds at all? Because they usually zig when shares zag, which makes the journey calmer and makes you more likely to stay invested. Notice what happens to the “lowest point” and “biggest fall” numbers when you move money between the two boxes — that is the trade you are actually making. But “usually” is doing real work in that sentence: in 2022 both fell together, shares −18% and bonds −25% after inflation. The simulation keeps shares and bonds from the same year precisely so that years like that stay possible.

        “Future euros” versus “today's buying power”

        €100 in ten years will not buy what €100 buys now. So the same outcome can be written as two different amounts, and the toggle next to your plan switches between them:

        • Future euros (the default) is the amount you would literally see printed on a statement in ten years — the number of euros you will be holding. It is the bigger of the two, because prices will have risen along with it.
        • Today's buying power takes that rise back out again. If it says €102 000, it means as much shopping as €102 000 buys you today. It is the smaller and more sobering figure, and it is the one the historical data gives us directly: everything is simulated in it and then converted.

        For the “future euros” view we do not guess inflation from history — the 1923 German hyperinflation would make that meaningless. Instead you set a single assumed inflation rate (2% by default, the European Central Bank's target), in the box beside the toggle. In that view we also assume you raise your monthly payment with inflation, so it keeps the same real value; that is why the “money you paid in” line is higher there.

        What the bands mean

        We do not run one future, we run a hundred thousand of them. Then we line up all hundred thousand results for each month and read off the ranking:

        • the middle outcome is the one right in the centre — half above, half below;
        • the everyday half is the middle 50, the range you would call unremarkable;
        • the 95 in 100 band leaves out the luckiest 2½ and unluckiest 2½;
        • the 99 in 100 band leaves out only the most extreme half-percent at each end.

        The middle outcome is deliberately used instead of the average. Averages get dragged upwards by a handful of spectacular runs and end up describing a future that most people will not get.

        The five steps behind every number on this page

        1. Collect the real history. Annual after-inflation returns for world shares and euro-area government bonds for 126 years, 1900–2025.
        2. Build one synthetic future. Pick a random year from history, then keep following the years that actually came after it for a while, then jump to another random year, and so on until the period is filled. Shares and bonds are always taken from the same year, so real crises stay intact: if 2008 comes up, you get 2008 for both.
        3. Fill in the months. A calendar year that ends flat can still have fallen 30% in the middle. Each year is broken into twelve monthly steps that wander realistically but are pinned to add up to exactly that year's real return. This is what makes the “lowest point” figures honest.
        4. Run your actual plan through it. Your starting amounts, your monthly payments added at each month's end, the yearly fund fees taken off — and, if you picked a country, the tax taken off as and when it falls due.
        5. Repeat a hundred thousand times and read off the ranking.

        How tax is handled

        Pick a country in the options box and every figure on this page becomes what you would actually walk away with: the balance you could sell for, after settling with the tax authority. Pick “nowhere” and you see the same plan before tax, which is the honest way to see how much tax is really costing you.

        The country changes nothing else. The two funds are the same wherever you live — a world share ETF and a euro government bond ETF — so the returns, the history and the resampling are identical. Only the tax differs.

        Money can be taken in three quite different ways, and they hurt differently:

        1. A yearly charge on the whole balance. Italy has one: the imposta di bollo (or IVAFE with a foreign broker), 0.20% a year of whatever your holding is worth. You owe it in good years and bad — it is not a tax on profit. The UK has nothing of the kind. Here it is charged in twelve small monthly slices, which is close to how brokers really bill it.
        2. A yearly tax on the income the funds earn. Shares pay dividends and bonds pay coupons. In the UK that income is taxable in the year it arises even if the fund keeps it and reinvests it — the “excess reportable income” of a reporting fund. In Italy an accumulating ETF is not touched at all until you sell, which is why the accumulating/distributing choice in the options box matters so much for an Italian investor. Income that has already been taxed is added to your cost base, so it is never taxed twice.
        3. A tax on the gain when you sell. Italy: 26% imposta sostitutiva, cut to 12.5% on the part of the gain that comes from white-list government bonds — so the bond part is taxed at roughly half the rate of the share part. The UK: capital gains tax at 18% inside the basic-rate band and 24% above it, with the first £3,000 of gain each year free.

        Every month the page shows the liquidation value: your balance minus the tax that selling in that month would trigger. That is why tax makes the “lowest point” and “biggest fall” figures worse too — the tax bill is real at every moment, not only at the end.

        Two details that are easy to get wrong, and are right here. First, tax is charged on the nominal gain: no country lets you deduct inflation. So the higher inflation is, the larger a share of your real profit the tax takes — switch the inflation assumption up and watch the tax bill grow even though nothing real has changed. Second, Italy taxes ETF gains as redditi di capitale but ETF losses as redditi diversi, two separate buckets: a loss on your bond fund therefore cannot be set against a gain on your share fund. In the UK it can. Try the two countries with a bond-heavy plan in a bad decade and you will see the difference.

        The tax is taken out of the pot. If in real life you would pay it from your current account instead, the arithmetic is the same — your total wealth ends up identical, the money just leaves from a different pocket.

        One thing no single number can show: the tax paid early costs you more than its face value, because that money can never compound again. That is why the drop in your final total is always bigger than the tax you actually handed over.

        Why not simply replay history?

        We do — that is section 7, and it is the most trustworthy thing on the page. But it cannot answer the question you asked. Between 1900 and 2025 there are only 117 ten-year stretches, and they overlap heavily: 1970–1979 and 1971–1980 share nine of their ten years. That is really only about a dozen independent experiments. You simply cannot measure a “1 in 100” event from a dozen samples.

        The resampling in step 2 solves that by reassembling real history into new orders. It reuses only things that really happened, but it can produce combinations we happened not to live through — a 2008 arriving right after a 1973, for instance. That is the point: nothing says the next decade must be one of the 117 we have already seen.

        Why stretches of about five years?

        If we shuffled single years independently, we would destroy something real: bad periods clump. Bond returns in particular are strongly related from one year to the next (a correlation of about +0.51 at one year's distance in this data, where anything below about 0.18 would be indistinguishable from chance), because interest-rate and inflation regimes last a long time. Shuffling years apart would quietly make the world look safer than it is.

        So we keep stretches with an average length of five years. Five is not a taste choice — it is the length at which the spread of simulated ten-year outcomes matches the spread actually seen in the real overlapping ten-year windows, to within about 1.6%. You can change it in the options box and watch the tails widen or narrow.

        What is not in these numbers

        • Most of the tax code. Tax is included for the two countries offered, but only the parts that a plain private investor holding two ETFs meets: the yearly charge on the balance, the tax on fund income, and the tax on the gain at sale. Wealth taxes elsewhere, inheritance tax, exit taxes on changing residence, the Italian financial transaction tax (which does not apply to ETFs anyway), losses carried forward from previous years and any allowance you may already have used elsewhere are all absent.
        • Trading and platform costs. The funds' yearly management fee is always charged, and a consultant's fee if you set one. Broker commissions, currency conversion and account-keeping fees are not.
        • Rebalancing. The two parts are left to drift apart. If shares boom, your risky share grows and stays grown.
        • Your behaviour. The simulation never panics, never stops paying in, never sells at the bottom. Real people do.
        • Currency risk. The world share index is treated in euro terms without hedging, which is what a typical euro investor holds — but exchange-rate history before 1971 was a very different world.
        • The unprecedented. Everything here is assembled from things that already happened. The next decade is allowed to be stranger than any of them.
        This is not financial or tax advice. It is a calculator for building intuition about risk. It cannot know your situation, your other savings, your job security, your debts or the rest of your tax position. Past returns genuinely do not predict future ones, and tax rules change.
        9 · Every word explained 23 terms, plainly
        ETF (exchange-traded fund)
        A single thing you can buy that holds many investments at once. A “world share ETF” holds shares in thousands of companies worldwide, so no single company failing can hurt you much. It trades like a share and typically costs 0.1–0.3% of your money per year.
        Government bond
        A loan to a government. You hand over money, receive interest, and get the money back at the end. Safer than shares, because governments rarely fail to pay — but the market price of an existing bond falls when new bonds start offering higher interest.
        Real return (“after inflation”)
        How much more you can actually buy. If your money grows 5% but prices rise 2%, your real return is about 3%. The whole simulation is run in real terms; switch the toggle to “today's buying power” and every figure on the page is a real one.
        Total return
        Price change plus the dividends and interest you received, assuming you reinvested them. Ignoring dividends would understate share returns by roughly 2–4% a year — so we never do.
        Percentile
        A position in the ranking. The “5th percentile” means: line up all outcomes from worst to best, and this is the one 5% of the way along. 95 out of 100 did better.
        Median (the “middle outcome”)
        The 50th percentile — the outcome exactly in the middle. More representative than the average, which a few enormous winners can pull far away from typical experience.
        Volatility
        How much returns bounce around from year to year. Higher volatility means a wider range of outcomes and a rougher ride, in both directions.
        Drawdown
        How far your balance has fallen from its own highest point so far. A 40% drawdown means that at some moment you were looking at 40% less than the most you had ever had.
        TER (total expense ratio)
        The fund's yearly fee, taken automatically out of the fund's value. 0.20% means €20 a year on €10 000. Small numbers, but they compound: 0.5% a year costs about 5% of your money over ten years.
        Consultant's fee (a percentage of what you hold)
        The commonest way advice and portfolio management are charged: a yearly percentage of everything in the account, not of the profit. 1% on €100 000 is €1 000 a year, owed whether the year was good or bad, and it grows as the pot grows. It behaves exactly like the TER above, only much larger — which is why it has its own section.
        Correlation
        Whether two things move together. +1 means always in lockstep, 0 means unrelated, −1 means perfect opposites. Shares and bonds have been roughly 0.2 here — related, but loosely, which is exactly what makes holding both useful.
        Monte Carlo simulation
        Running the same plan through an enormous number of possible futures and then studying the whole collection of results, rather than trying to guess the single right answer.
        Accumulating vs distributing fund
        An accumulating fund keeps the dividends and interest it receives and reinvests them for you, so your holding simply becomes worth more. A distributing fund pays the cash into your account. The investments are identical; only the plumbing differs — and in Italy that plumbing decides whether you are taxed every year or only when you sell.
        Imposta di bollo / IVAFE (Italy)
        A yearly charge of 0.20% on the value of your investments — €20 a year on €10 000 — regardless of whether you made money. Charged by your bank if the account is Italian (imposta di bollo) or declared by you if the broker is foreign (IVAFE). It is the one tax you pay even in a losing year.
        Imposta sostitutiva (Italy)
        The flat tax on investment gains, 26%, which replaces income tax on them. Reduced to 12.5% on the part of the return that comes from government bonds of countries on Italy's “white list” — which is why the bond part of this plan is taxed more gently than the share part.
        White list (Italy)
        The official list of countries whose government bonds get the reduced 12.5% rate rather than 26%. It includes all euro-area governments, so a euro government bond ETF that really holds the bonds qualifies.
        Capital gains tax (UK)
        Tax on the profit when you sell, not on the money you invested. 18% if your income keeps you in the basic-rate band, 24% above it, and the first £3,000 of gains in a tax year is free. Losses can be set against gains — which is not true in Italy.
        Excess reportable income (UK)
        The income a fund earned but did not pay out. If a fund has UK “reporting status”, you owe tax on that income in the year it arose even though you never saw the cash — and the tax you paid is then added to your cost base so the same money is not taxed again when you sell.
        ISA (UK)
        An Individual Savings Account: an ordinary investment account inside a tax-free wrapper. No tax on the income, no tax on the gains, nothing to declare. There is a yearly limit on how much you may pay in, which this page does not check.
        Cost base (or cost basis)
        What the tax authority treats as the amount you paid. Your taxable gain is what you sell for minus your cost base. Every euro you contribute raises it, and so does any income you have already been taxed on — that is the mechanism that stops the same money being taxed twice.
        Nominal gain
        The gain measured in plain euros, ignoring inflation. Tax is always charged on this, never on the smaller real gain — so inflation quietly increases the true tax rate on your purchasing power.
        Bootstrap / block bootstrap
        Building new possible histories by drawing pieces out of the real one. “Block” means we draw multi-year stretches instead of single years, so that patterns lasting several years survive.
        10 · Where the data comes from sources, weaknesses, how to check it

        126 years, 1900–2025. Dataset built on —.

        1900 – 2020

        The Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (release 6), the standard academic source for long-run financial history. It gives yearly equity total returns, long government bond total returns and consumer prices for 18 advanced economies.

        • World shares = the average real equity return across the 16 countries that report equity data, weighted by each country's economy size.
        • Euro bonds = the same calculation across Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Portugal and Finland.
        • Weights use the previous year's economy size, so the calculation never uses information it could not have had at the time.
        • Real return per country = (1 + that country's total return) ÷ (1 + that country's inflation) − 1.

        2021 – present

        Actual investable funds, so the recent past reflects what you could really have bought: iShares Core MSCI World UCITS ETF (IWDA.AS) in euros for shares, iShares Core EUR Govt Bond UCITS ETF (IEGA.AS) for bonds, both on dividend-adjusted daily closing prices, deflated by the euro-area HICP inflation index from Eurostat.

        Honest weaknesses in the data

        • Economy-size weights, not market-size weights. Nobody has reliable stock-market sizes for every country back to 1900, so the world index is weighted by GDP. This is standard practice in long-horizon research, but it is not what a real index fund does today.
        • Yearly data, not daily. Before 2020 we only have one number per year. The month-by-month detail is reconstructed statistically, not observed.
        • Only countries that survived as data providers. The dataset covers advanced economies that kept records. Markets that were closed or destroyed — Russia in 1917, China in 1949 — are absent, which makes history look slightly kinder than it was.
        • The join in 2021. Two different kinds of measurement are stitched together. They were checked against each other on the years where both exist, and they agree closely, but a join is a join.
        • The euro did not exist for most of this history. “Euro government bonds” before 1999 means the bonds of the countries that later formed the euro, in their own currencies.
